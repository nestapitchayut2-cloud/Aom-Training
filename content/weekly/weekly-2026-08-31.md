## 📖 Reading (20 นาที จับเวลา · 12 ข้อ)

**บทความ (แต่งขึ้นในสไตล์ IELTS Academic — history of science):**

### The Discovery of Insulin

**A.** For centuries, a diagnosis of what physicians called "sugar sickness" was effectively a death sentence. Ancient writers in Egypt, India and Greece described patients who lost weight rapidly, produced enormous quantities of urine and suffered an unquenchable thirst. The Greek physician Aretaeus, writing in the second century, gave the condition the name "diabetes", from a word meaning "to pass through", because sufferers seemed to pass water straight through their bodies. The sweetness of the urine, noticed by physicians who tasted it, led much later to the fuller term "diabetes mellitus", *mellitus* being Latin for "honeyed". Yet naming the disease brought physicians no closer to treating it. Until the twentieth century, the only therapy on offer was a near-starvation diet that might extend a young patient's life by a year or two while leaving them weak and emaciated.

**B.** During the second half of the nineteenth century, laboratory research began to narrow down the origin of the disease. In 1889, two researchers in Strasbourg, Oskar Minkowski and Joseph von Mering, removed the pancreas from a dog in order to study digestion. They noticed that the animal, previously house-trained, now urinated frequently and uncontrollably. Testing the urine, Minkowski found it loaded with sugar. The experiment showed that the pancreas produced something that kept blood sugar under control, and that without the organ an animal rapidly developed severe diabetes. Attention turned to clusters of cells within the pancreas, first described by the German medical student Paul Langerhans in 1869 and later named the "islets of Langerhans". Researchers suspected that these islets released a substance directly into the blood, but every attempt to extract it in active form failed, because the digestive juices of the pancreas destroyed the substance before it could be isolated.

**C.** The breakthrough came in Toronto. In 1920, Frederick Banting, a young Canadian surgeon with no research reputation and a struggling medical practice, read an article about the pancreas and conceived an idea for protecting the islet secretion from the digestive juices. He approached John Macleod, professor of physiology at the University of Toronto, who was sceptical but eventually lent Banting laboratory space, ten dogs and a student assistant, Charles Best, chosen by the toss of a coin. Through the summer of 1921, Banting and Best tied off the pancreatic ducts of dogs, waited for the digestive part of the organ to waste away, and then prepared extracts from the surviving islet tissue. Injected into a dog whose pancreas had been removed, the extract lowered its blood sugar and briefly revived it.

**D.** The early extracts were impure and caused painful abscesses. Macleod brought in a skilled biochemist, James Collip, who developed a purification method using alcohol of varying strengths. In January 1922, a purified extract was given to Leonard Thompson, a fourteen-year-old boy dying of diabetes in a Toronto hospital. The first injection, from a batch prepared before Collip's improvements, had little effect. A second, purer dose given days later brought his blood sugar down sharply and cleared the sugar from his urine. Thompson recovered and lived for another thirteen years. News of the result spread quickly, and desperate families began travelling to Toronto from across North America and Europe.

**E.** The University of Toronto could not produce enough extract to meet demand, and the team signed an agreement with the American pharmaceutical company Eli Lilly to manufacture it on an industrial scale. Within a year, the drug, now called insulin, was being shipped in large quantities. The patent was sold to the university for one dollar each by Banting, Best and Collip, who believed that a life-saving treatment should not be a source of private profit. In 1923, the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to Banting and Macleod. Banting, angry that Best had been overlooked, shared his prize money with him; Macleod shared his with Collip.

**F.** Insulin transformed diabetes from a fatal disease into a manageable long-term condition, but it was never a cure. Patients still needed injections for the rest of their lives, and early preparations acted so quickly that blood sugar was difficult to keep stable. Later decades brought slow-acting forms, more purified animal insulins and, from the 1980s, human insulin produced by genetically modified bacteria. Researchers today continue to work towards treatments that would restore the body's own insulin production. For the millions of people who inject insulin every day, the work carried out in a cramped Toronto laboratory over a single year remains the foundation of their survival.

### 💊 สรุปคลินิก — Type 2 diabetes & insulin safety

**Concept level:**
- **Type 2 diabetes** = insulin resistance plus a relative shortfall in insulin production. Management is stepwise: lifestyle measures first, then oral therapy (metformin is the usual first-line agent), with injectable therapies — including insulin — added when glycaemic targets are not being met.
- **Insulin preparations are grouped by how fast and how long they act:** rapid-acting and short-acting (used at meals and for correction), intermediate-acting, and long-acting basal analogues; premixed products combine two of these.
- **Insulin is a high-risk ("high-alert") medicine** — the main hazard is hypoglycaemia, which can be severe or fatal.

**Key safety points (exam-level, generic):**
- Always write the word **"units" in full** — the abbreviation "U" is easily misread as a zero, causing a 10-fold overdose.
- Use **insulin-specific syringes / the device's own pen** only; never draw insulin up in an IV syringe on the ward except for a prepared infusion.
- **Look-alike / sound-alike names and devices** are a common error source — confirm the exact insulin, strength and device.
- **Rotate injection sites.** Repeated injection into one spot causes **lipohypertrophy**, which makes absorption slow and unpredictable.
- **Hypoglycaemia:** recognise it (sweating, tremor, hunger, palpitations, confusion). In a conscious patient, treat with **15–20 g of fast-acting carbohydrate**, recheck blood glucose after **15 minutes**, repeat if still low, then give a longer-acting carbohydrate snack.
- **Sick-day rules:** never stop insulin during acute illness, even if eating little; check glucose (and ketones) more often and keep up fluid intake.
- **Driving:** hypoglycaemia and reduced hypo awareness make driving unsafe; advise the patient to follow the licensing rules that apply to them and not to drive while hypos are uncontrolled.

### 🗣️ Counselling Card — nocturnal hypoglycaemia on basal insulin

> **Ness plays:** Khun A, 58, type 2 diabetes for 9 years. On metformin plus a **long-acting basal insulin once at night**. Comes to the pharmacy for advice.
>
> **Opening line:** *"For the past week I've been waking up at about 4 or 5 in the morning feeling shaky and sweaty — two or three times. I ate a couple of biscuits and it passed."*
>
> **If asked about the dose:** a nurse increased the night-time insulin "a few weeks ago"; last night his glucose was **6 mmol/L** before the dose.
> **If asked about food:** he is trying to lose weight and has been **skipping his evening meal** on some days.
> **If asked about daytime readings:** mostly **7–9 mmol/L**, "pretty good".
> **If asked what he does about it:** goes back to sleep after the biscuits, hasn't told anyone, still **drives to work**.

**Marking criteria:**
1. **Recognises the pattern as nocturnal hypoglycaemia** and treats it as a safety problem that needs action now — not something to reassure away.
2. **Makes and justifies a clinical decision to refer back to the prescriber promptly for a basal dose review**, with the reasoning stated: repeated night-time hypos + a pre-dose glucose already near normal + skipped evening meals together mean the basal dose is now too high for his current intake.
3. **Gives correct immediate self-management advice:** proper hypo treatment (15–20 g fast-acting carbohydrate, recheck at 15 min, then a snack), keep fast-acting carbohydrate by the bed, check a glucose reading during an episode if able, and **do not skip the evening meal** while on this dose.
4. **Addresses driving safety:** repeated hypos with reduced awareness make driving dangerous; advise he should not drive while hypos are uncontrolled and must follow the licensing rules that apply to him.

## Pharmacy Core — เฉลยคำนวณ

1. **135 mg/dL** — 7.5 mmol/L × 18 mg/dL = 135 mg/dL
2. **36 units/day** — 72 kg × 0.5 units/kg = 36 units
3. **6 units per meal** — total 36 units → basal = 50% = 18 units; mealtime total = 18 units ÷ 3 = 6 units each
4. **3 mL/hour** — 50 units in 50 mL = 1 unit/mL, so 3 units/hour = 3 mL/hour
5. **3 units** — (16 − 7) = 9 mmol/L above target; 9 ÷ 3 = 3 units exactly (no rounding needed)

> 💡 ข้อ 4 เป็นเหตุผลที่ standard IV insulin infusion มักผสมเป็น **1 unit/mL** เสมอ — เลข unit/hour = เลข mL/hour ลดโอกาสคำนวณพลาดตอนเร่งด่วน
